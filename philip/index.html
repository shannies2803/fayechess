The honest advice first

What actually wins scholastic games

1

Stop hanging pieces

Most games under 1400 are decided by someone leaving a piece where it can just be taken. Before every single move, look at what your opponent's last move now attacks.

2

Know three mating patterns cold

Back rank, the two-rook ladder, and queen-plus-king. If you can't finish, a winning position is only a draw.

3

Have one answer to 1.e4 and one to 1.d4

You don't need a repertoire. You need to not be lost on move four. Pick one, learn six moves deep, play it a hundred times.

4

Write your moves down

It's required in most rated events, and it slows you down at exactly the moments you're about to blunder. The scoresheet next to every board here is the real thing.

5

Use your clock

Losing on time in a winning position is the most painful way to lose. Check the clock every five moves, not every thirty.

6

Play the whole game

Kids resign far too early and stop concentrating when ahead. Opponents at your level blunder back constantly. Sit there and make them prove it.

    Mating patterns

    Strong players don't calculate these — they recognise them. Each diagram shows the position one move before mate. Press the button to play it, and learn the shape rather than the moves.

    Why patterns beat calculation

    A grandmaster and a beginner calculate at roughly the same speed. The difference is that the grandmaster has tens of thousands of these shapes stored, so a position that the beginner has to work out move by move, the strong player simply sees. Every pattern you learn here is one you never have to calculate again.

    What this is, and what it isn't

    About this site

    A free chess trainer for young players preparing for competition. One file, no account, nothing to install. Everything below is worth two minutes before you rely on it.

    Your privacy

    Nothing leaves your device

    • No account, no sign-up, no email address.
    • No analytics, no tracking, no advertising.
    • Nothing you do here is uploaded anywhere. There is no server to upload it to.
    • Your progress is stored by your own browser, on your own device.

    The only thing loaded from the internet is the typeface. Everything else — the chess engine, all the positions, every lesson — is inside this one file and works with the network switched off.

    Your progress

    Saved on this device only

    Because there is no account, progress does not follow you between devices, and it is tied to the exact web address you opened the site from. A different phone, or the same site at a different address, starts fresh.

    Use the Back up button in the sidebar before changing device or clearing your browser. It saves a small file you can restore with the button next to it.

    The sidebar always shows whether saving is working. If it says "not saved — this session only", your browser is blocking storage, usually private browsing mode.

    Being straight with you

    What the computer here can and cannot do

    The practice opponent

    A small chess engine written for this site. It plays legal, sensible chess and knows the openings in the Openings section, so it will play real theory for the first several moves. It is not Stockfish or anything like it. A strong club player will beat it.

    Analyse your game

    A fast blunder-check, not deep analysis. It reliably catches hung pieces and short tactics — the things that actually decide junior games — and it will miss subtle positional mistakes. It also does not judge the opening moves, because a shallow check flags perfectly normal opening play as wrong.

    The positions

    Every puzzle, drill, mating pattern and opening line in this site was replayed through the engine. Mates are proved to be forced against every defence, not just against one line. Opening lines are checked move by move for legality.

    The master games

    The game scores were reconstructed and then verified. Ten of the eleven confirm themselves, because they end in a checkmate or a repetition that the engine checks — a misremembered move would break that. The exception is Fischer–Spassky 1972, which ends in resignation: its 81 moves are all legal, which makes it very probably right, but it cannot prove itself the way the others do.

    Using it away from the internet

    Save the page and it runs offline. On a phone or tablet, open it in the browser and choose Add to Home Screen — it then behaves like an app, which is the easiest way for a child to use it at a tournament.

    If the typeface looks different offline, that is the only thing that needs the network. Nothing else changes.

    If something looks wrong

    Pieces too big or too small for your screen? There is an S / M / L control in the sidebar — symbol fonts differ a lot between devices and no single size suits them all.

    Puzzles are graded 1 to 5 by a measured difficulty score — how many candidate moves there are, whether the key move is a quiet one, how long the solution runs, and how cluttered the board is. Levels 1 and 2 are warm-ups; a tournament player should live at 3 and 4. Puzzle rush keeps a run inside one band so the difficulty does not jump around.

    Too much material? Set a training plan on the home page and switch on Focus mode. It hides everything except your three chosen openings and the material that matches your level, and it always tells you what it has hidden.

    Worth memorising

    What the pieces are worth

    These are guides, not laws. A knight sitting in a hole in the enemy position beats a bishop with nowhere to go, and in the endgame a rook pawn can be worth more than either.

    Between rounds

    Losing well

    Shake hands, then leave the room

    Say "good game" whatever happened. Then get outside, get water, get a snack. Do not sit and stew in the hall — that's how one loss turns into three.

    Look at it later, not now

    Keep your scoresheet. That's what it's for. Going over a loss the same evening with a coach or a computer is where nearly all your rating comes from.

    The next round is a new game

    Nobody at any level wins every round. The players who finish high aren't the ones who never lose, they're the ones who don't lose twice in a row.
